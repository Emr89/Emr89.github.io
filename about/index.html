My path

Statistics gave structure to the way I already liked to think.

I originally became interested in quantitative work through psychology. What hooked me was not simply calculating an answer — it was the idea that questions about people, behavior, performance, and uncertainty could be made more understandable through careful measurement.

That curiosity eventually led me to major in Statistics at Rutgers University, while keeping psychology as a minor. Statistics gave me a language for something I had always enjoyed: breaking complicated problems into relationships, assumptions, signals, and decisions.

Sports became the perfect laboratory.

Team sports fascinated me long before I knew what a regression model was. Football and soccer in particular are systems where many people make interconnected decisions at the same time, often under severe time pressure. A small difference in spacing, timing, role, or strategy can change everything.

That made sports analytics feel natural to me. Rather than asking only what happened?, I became increasingly interested in questions like why did the structure of the play make that outcome possible? and what could a team do differently next time?

That line of thinking eventually led to my NFL Big Data Bowl work, where I used player-tracking data to study kick returns at the instant the returner received the ball. The project developed through an NFL mentorship experience and became one of the clearest examples of the kind of work I enjoy: combining technical modeling with football interpretation and actionable questions.

Analytics

Measure relationships, not just isolated numbers.

I’m especially interested in spatial structure, interaction effects, latent patterns, prediction, and turning model output into useful interpretation.

Strategy

Analysis should help someone decide something.

The most interesting question usually comes after the model: what does the result change about how we understand or approach the problem?

Systems

Complexity is often easier to understand as a whole.

Projects such as CFA reflect my interest in connecting analytics, simulation, presentation, technology, and user experience instead of treating them as separate problems.

Growth

Old work should become better work.

I like revisiting earlier projects, identifying what I would do differently now, and treating improvement as evidence of learning rather than something to hide.

What I work with

My technical work has included R, Python, SQL, SAS, machine learning, statistical modeling, data visualization, tracking data, multivariate analysis, and predictive analytics. I’ve also increasingly worked with simulation, 3D visualization, AI-assisted development, and product-oriented analytical systems.

I’m currently most interested in opportunities where I can contribute to sports analytics, data analytics, statistics, modeling, decision support, or analytical product development.

Outside the model

I’m naturally curious well beyond analytics. I enjoy football, soccer and hockey, history — especially Ancient Rome — thoughtful science fiction and fantasy, creative writing, music, mindfulness, travel, and building side projects simply because an idea is interesting enough to chase.

That broader curiosity is why this portfolio also includes Futurezen and the Lab. Not every worthwhile idea needs to begin as a formal analytics project.

The thread connecting all of it

Understand the system. Find the relationships that matter. Build something useful from what you learn.
