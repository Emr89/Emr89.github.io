Finding Kick-Return Structure
at the Moment of Reception

Can one snapshot of a kick return — taken when the returner receives the ball — already tell us something meaningful about how many yards the play will produce?

Executive summary

The play had not unfolded yet. The structure already mattered.

My goal was not simply to predict kick-return yardage. I wanted to understand whether the geometry and movement of the players at the instant of reception contained an early signal about what the play would become.

The project began as an NFL Big Data Bowl analysis and developed through an NFL mentorship experience. I engineered spatial relationships from tracking data, compared multiple model families, and then explored a space-time-aware validation approach with Random Forests.

The strongest reported model reached an R² of 0.529. More important to me than the number itself was the football pattern behind it: relative spacing and coverage-unit structure repeatedly carried more signal than isolated movement statistics such as individual speed or orientation.

The central idea

Instead of evaluating a kickoff only by where the return ended, ask what the arrangement of all 22 players already tells us when the return begins.

The question

Why freeze the play at reception?

Kick returns are unusually useful for studying interaction because every player is moving through open space toward a rapidly changing common objective.

By freezing each return at the event where the kick returner receives the ball, I could ask a cleaner question: how much of the eventual return is associated with the state of the system before most of the return itself has happened?

The target was kick-return yardage. The explanatory information came primarily from NFL tracking data, with play, game, and PFF scouting data used to identify the correct players, teams, events, and contextual fields.

Method

Turn tracking coordinates into football relationships.

The most important work happened before model fitting: restructuring the tracking data so each play described meaningful relationships rather than a loose collection of player rows.

Step 01
Normalize the field Flip coordinates so plays moving left and right share one consistent direction.
Step 02
Freeze reception Subset each kick return to the event where the returner receives the ball.
Step 03
Engineer relationships Create Euclidean distance, ranked proximity, unit spread, and movement summaries.
Step 04
Compare models Test linear, regularized, neural-network, and Random Forest approaches, then add space-time-aware resampling.

Feature engineering

I created player-to-returner Euclidean distances, kept exact coverage positions such as L1–L5 and R1–R5, ranked other players by proximity, and summarized groups using totals, means, and standard deviations. For the kicking team, I also built analogous summaries for speed, acceleration, orientation, and direction.

This was an early version of an idea I still find important in sports analytics: the representation of the interaction can matter more than the complexity of the algorithm.

Model development

The validation strategy changed the result.

My initial model comparison tested several common regression and machine-learning approaches after centering, scaling, and Yeo-Johnson transformation where appropriate.

In those early comparisons, the strongest Random Forest and linear-model variants reached roughly R² ≈ 0.35, while regularized approaches clustered closer to roughly 0.30.

Later in the project I introduced the CAST package and a space-time-aware resampling approach alongside Random Forest modeling. That reported model used only seven predictors and reached R² = 0.529.

Important statistical wording

R² = 0.529 means the model explained 52.9% of the observed variance under the reported evaluation setup. It does not mean the predictions were “52.9% accurate.”

Football findings

Spacing kept winning the argument.

Across the exploratory model variants, the strongest recurring signals were not simply “who is fastest?” They described how the coverage and return units were arranged relative to the returner and to one another.

Finding 01

The leading coverage distance mattered.

The mean distance of the two closest kicking-team players emerged as one of the most influential engineered measures.

Finding 02

Spatial variables outweighed many movement variables.

Euclidean-position features repeatedly carried more importance than individual orientation and speed measures in the exploratory models.

Finding 03

Coverage looked like a unit problem.

Elite pursuit speed mattered, but the rest of the coverage team also needed to remain within a useful range of proximity. Mean team acceleration also appeared meaningful, suggesting coordinated movement rather than isolated athleticism.

Finding 04

Blocker geometry mattered too.

For the receiving team, the spread of blocker distances and the positioning of the two closest blockers carried useful signal.

The practical football interpretation was not “speed does not matter.” It was more nuanced: speed matters inside a spatial system. A fast coverage player arriving alone is different from a fast coverage player arriving as part of a well-spaced, compressing unit.

What I would improve now

The project is more useful when I show what I learned from its limitations.

This was one of my first serious tracking-data projects. Looking back with more experience, there are several things I would tighten before treating the model as production-grade football analysis.

Use play-grouped / space-time validation from the beginning. The later CAST-based approach should be part of the initial experimental design rather than something discovered near the end.
Strictly separate prediction-time information from post-play information. Some exploratory feature sets considered outcome-adjacent variables such as missed tackles. A modern reception-time model should exclude anything that is not actually known at reception and reserve those fields for post hoc analysis.
Treat importance as association, not causation. Variable importance can identify useful signals, but it does not prove that changing one feature would cause the predicted change in return yardage.
Model interactions more explicitly. I would now explore graph-based or permutation-invariant player representations, pressure / lane geometry, pursuit angles, blocker leverage, and uncertainty around expected return yards.

This retrospective is part of why I wanted to revamp the portfolio. The goal is not to make older work look as if it were done yesterday. It is to show what was genuinely interesting, what was technically accomplished, and how I would make the work stronger now.

Why this project still matters to me

It changed the kinds of questions I wanted to ask.

The biggest takeaway was not a specific model score. It was realizing how much information lives in the relationships between players — and how much football analysis can gain when we model the structure of the play rather than treating every player statistic independently.

That idea shows up again in later work throughout this portfolio, especially in my interest in strategy, simulation, tracking systems, and CFA.
